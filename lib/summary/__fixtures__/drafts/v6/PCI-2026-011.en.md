## Why you were in hospital

You had a heart attack, called ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). This happens when an artery that feeds the heart muscle becomes blocked. You were admitted as an emergency on 2026-09-24 and went home on 2026-09-27.

## What was done

Your doctor reached your heart arteries through a small tube placed in the artery of your right wrist (radial access). Dye was used to take X-ray pictures of the arteries (angiogram). The blocked artery, the proximal LCX (left circumflex artery, first part), was opened with balloons. One drug-eluting stent, a small mesh tube that releases medicine to keep the artery open, was placed there.

## What we found

- LMCA (left main coronary artery): 0% narrowing.
- LAD (left anterior descending artery): 0% narrowing.
- RCA (right coronary artery): 10% narrowing.
- Proximal LCX: 100% narrowing, meaning it was completely blocked. After the stent, 5% narrowing remained and blood flow was normal.

## Your heart pump

Your heart's pumping strength (LVEF) was 55%.

DAPT means dual antiplatelet therapy: Aspirin and Ticagrelor are two tablets taken together that stop clots forming in the stent. Do not stop these medicines without asking your cardiologist.

## Looking after yourself

- Take part in a heart rehabilitation programme.
- Stop smoking. This is one of the most important things you can do for your heart.
- Take your DAPT tablets regularly, exactly as prescribed.

## When to come back urgently

Go to the nearest hospital emergency department or call 108 immediately if you have:

- Chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, or coming back
- Severe breathlessness
- Fainting or collapse
- Bleeding from the wrist puncture site that does not stop after 10 minutes of firm pressure
- A rapidly growing swelling at the puncture site
- The hand on that side becoming cold, pale, numb or very painful
- Black or bloody stools, or vomiting blood (you are on blood-thinning tablets)

Contact the cardiology team within a day for:

- Increasing pain, redness, warmth or discharge at the puncture site
- Fever
